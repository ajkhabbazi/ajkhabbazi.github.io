The thesis

My PhD in three phases

Phase 1 · Field evidence What real buildings have shown About 4% of advanced control papers test in a real building. 71% of the tests use potentially unreliable protocols. The other 29% report 15–22% energy savings. Completed2023–2025

Simulation studies suggest model predictive control and reinforcement learning can cut costs, emissions, and grid stress, yet adoption remains limited. I led a team from four institutions in reviewing all 104 known field studies, 24 in homes and 80 in commercial buildings, and evaluating their protocols and reported outcomes. Only 13 studies reported what deployment cost. We made five recommendations for future field research, including more rigorous protocols and open reporting of costs.

2
Phase 2 · Savings measurement Measuring savings reliably Tests that control only part of a building overstate energy savings, sometimes several times over, because heat transfer from neighboring zones goes uncounted. 2025–present

The review pointed to one common source of error. Many field tests control a few zones in a larger building and report savings for those zones alone. We proved that this overstates whole-building savings, because the heat drawn from neighboring zones is paid for by their equipment and isn’t counted. The error grows when the zones are poorly insulated from each other and when the temperature difference between them is larger. We also derived a correction that needs no baseline estimate, and it recovered the true savings in every simulated case. Next, we aim to confirm this in a laboratory experiment.

Savings overstatement falls as the controlled zone has more exterior walls, and grows with β.

A season simulation of a two-story house with control on the first floor only. Perceived savings exceed true savings by 65% to 190%.

3
Phase 3 · Control algorithms Control that learns from building data Developing learning-based control that needs little data and engineering effort, and testing it on reducing the peak cooling demand of the Purdue campus. 2026–present

The first two phases asked what advanced control has delivered in real buildings and how to measure it. This phase turns to the control itself. Advanced control is still costly to set up in each new building, which keeps it from spreading. I am developing learning-based control algorithms that need little data and engineering, so they can move from one building to many. With Purdue’s Energy and Utilities team, I am testing them in simulation on campus buildings served by the central chilled water plant, where the peak load sets how much plant capacity the campus needs.

Before the PhD

Master’s research

Hydrogen in natural gas pipelines Bottom-side injection mixes in a pipe length about four times shorter than horizontal injection and five times shorter than top-side injection. Master’s thesis, UBC Completed2021–2023

Canada has committed to net-zero emissions by 2050. One way to cut emissions from natural gas is to blend renewable hydrogen into the pipelines already in the ground. The catch is that hydrogen is about eight times lighter than natural gas, so when injected from the top it stays trapped along the top wall instead of mixing in. In an NSERC-funded collaboration between FortisBC and UBC, I used CFD with ideal and real gas models to find where and how to inject it. Narrowing the injection pipe cut the distance needed to mix from 143 to 38 pipe diameters, because the faster jet reaches deeper into the main flow. Among injection directions, bottom-side injection won, because the hydrogen rises through the natural gas and mixes on its way up.
