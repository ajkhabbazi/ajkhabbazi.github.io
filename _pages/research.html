Phase 1 · Field demonstrations What real buildings have shown 71% of field demonstrations rely on potentially unreliable protocols. Completed · 2023–2025

Simulation studies suggest model predictive control and reinforcement learning can cut costs, emissions, and grid stress, yet adoption remains limited. I led a team from four institutions in reviewing all known real-world demonstrations, evaluating their protocols and reported outcomes, and we recommended ways to improve scientific practice in the field.

Phase 2 · Control scope Measuring savings reliably When only interior zones are controlled, all perceived savings are fictitious. In progress · 2025–present

The review pointed to one common source of error. Many field tests control a few zones in a larger building and report savings for those zones alone. We proved that this overstates whole-building savings, because heat drawn from neighboring zones isn’t counted, and used simulations to show how large the error can be. We also developed a correction that needs no baseline estimate. Next, we aim to show this in an experiment.

Savings overstatement falls as the controlled zone has more exterior walls, and grows with β.

A season simulation of a two-story house with control on the first floor only. Perceived savings exceed true savings by 65% to 190%.

Phase 3 · Peak demand Cutting the campus cooling peak Simulating building control that lowers the peak load on the campus chilled water plant. In progress · 2026–present

The first two phases asked what advanced control has delivered in real buildings and how to measure it. This phase applies those lessons to the campus. With Purdue’s Energy and Utilities team, I am simulating control of campus buildings served by the central chilled water plant, with the aim of lowering the plant’s peak load. The goal is a method that needs little data and engineering to set up and can move from one building to many.

Control with almost no commissioning Up to 87% of optimal savings under time-varying pricing, in simulation. In progress · 2026–present

Advanced controllers are costly to commission, which limits their use in real buildings. We developed two nearly commissioning-free controllers for objectives such as energy costs and pollution, needing at most two thermal parameters.

Data-driven predictive control The lowest prediction error and failure rate of the predictors tested, especially with limited training data. In progress · 2026–present

Data-driven predictive control can skip much of the modeling work, but its performance depends on how future behavior is predicted from data. We introduced a state-space predictor that connects this approach to established predictive control theory.

Prediction error, control failure rate, and cost as training data grows.

Home power control Coordinated control cut peak current by up to 70% while keeping homes comfortable in nearly all scenarios. Completed · 2025–2026

Electric heating, water heating, and EV charging can overload 100–200 A panels, and upgrades cost $2k–$10k. We built a simulation framework to compare control strategies across electrification scenarios.

Smart Buildings Simulator (SBSim) Co-leading a benchmark of how fast control agents train in SBSim versus EnergyPlus. Open-source contributor In progress · 2025–present

Google built SBSim, an open-source simulator, to train reinforcement learning agents that control heating and cooling in office buildings. I work on it in an open-source working group with researchers from Google and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. Our benchmark times agent training in SBSim against EnergyPlus, the U.S. Department of Energy’s building energy simulator. I also proposed the predictive control workstream, gave two talks to the group (April and October 2026), contributed code, and reviewed pull requests.

Hydrogen in natural gas pipelines Bottom-side injection mixes in a pipe length about four times shorter than horizontal injection and five times shorter than top-side injection. Master’s thesis, UBC Completed · 2021–2023

Canada has committed to net-zero emissions by 2050. One way to cut emissions from natural gas is to blend renewable hydrogen into the pipelines already in the ground. The catch is that hydrogen is about eight times lighter than natural gas, so when injected from the top it stays trapped along the top wall instead of mixing in. In an NSERC-funded collaboration between FortisBC and UBC, I used CFD with ideal and real gas models to find where and how to inject it. Bottom-side injection won, because the hydrogen rises through the natural gas and mixes on its way up.
