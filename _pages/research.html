Buildings use about a third of the world’s energy, and much of that depends on how their systems are controlled. Advanced control, such as predictive control and reinforcement learning, can save buildings energy and money, but it rarely reaches real buildings at scale. My research aims to advance the state of the art of practical automation systems for efficient, occupant- and grid-interactive buildings. I focus on minimizing the data and engineering needed to achieve good, if imperfect, performance, and on measuring the results reliably.

Phase 1 · State of the art What real buildings have shown 71% of field demonstrations rely on potentially unreliable protocols. Completed

Simulation studies suggest predictive control and reinforcement learning can cut costs, emissions, and grid stress, yet adoption remains limited. I led a team from four institutions in reviewing all known real-world demonstrations, evaluating their protocols and reported outcomes, and we recommended ways to improve scientific practice in the field.

Phase 2 · Protocols Measuring savings reliably When only interior zones are controlled, all perceived savings are fictitious. In progress

The review pointed to one common source of error. Many field tests control a few zones in a larger building and report savings for those zones alone. We proved this overstates whole-building savings, because heat drawn from neighboring zones isn’t counted, and developed a correction that needs no baseline estimate.

{% bibliography --group_by none --query @*[research=savings]* %}
Phase 3 · Deployment Control that scales Developing control that needs little setup and testing it in an occupied campus building. In progress

The first two phases asked what advanced control has delivered in real buildings and how to measure it. This phase puts those lessons to work. With Purdue’s Energy and Utilities team, I am developing control that needs little data and engineering to set up, and testing it in an occupied campus building served by the central chilled water plant. The goal is a method that can move from one building to many.

Control with almost no commissioning Up to 87% of optimal savings under time-varying pricing, in simulation. In progress

Advanced controllers are costly to commission, which limits their use in real buildings. We developed two nearly commissioning-free controllers for objectives such as energy costs and pollution, needing at most two thermal parameters.

Data-driven predictive control The lowest prediction error and failure rate of the predictors tested, especially with limited training data. In progress

Data-driven predictive control can skip much of the modeling work, but its performance depends on how future behavior is predicted from data. We introduced a state-space predictor that connects this approach to established predictive control theory.

{% bibliography --group_by none --query @*[research=tpc]* %}
Home power control Coordinated control cut peak current by up to 70% while keeping homes comfortable in nearly all scenarios. In progress

Electric heating, water heating, and EV charging can overload 100–200 A panels, and upgrades cost $2k–$10k. We built a simulation framework to compare control strategies across electrification scenarios.

Hydrogen in natural gas pipelines Bottom-side injection mixes in a pipe length about four times shorter than horizontal injection and five times shorter than top-side injection. Master’s thesis, UBC Completed

Hydrogen is buoyant, so when blended into natural gas pipelines it tends to stratify and collect near the top wall. At UBC, funded by FortisBC, I used CFD with ideal and real gas models to study how injection design affects mixing.
